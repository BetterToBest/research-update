Sharing rules:
- 'pay': x_i ∝ E_ik × 2(1-F_i), F_i rank in cash income over cost of living (0 least able, 1 most). So weight = 2(1-F_i). For least able F=0 => weight 2; for most able F=1 => weight 0. Linear in rank. Is that defensible? It's a linear approximation to a market rationing by ability to pay. But actual market rationing is not linear in rank; it's determined by willingness/ability to pay, which depends on marginal utility, prices, and possibly credit. A linear weight from 2 to 0 means the top half (F>0.5) has weight <1, bottom half >1; the very richest get zero cut. But if the cut is small relative to total spending, even the rich could be cut a little; if cut is large, the poor may be cut beyond their spending? They say no household cut beyond its own spending on sector. The normalization ensures sum x_i = cut. But the linear incidence may not match any actual market: in a shortage, those with higher income can pay higher prices, but if prices don't clear (θ price rise may not clear), the shortfall may be allocated by queueing, waiting, or seller discretion, not proportional to income rank. Also F_i is rank in cash income over cost of living, not wealth or ability to pay; a wealthy household with low current income could be ranked low and cut heavily. Better: use ability to pay = income + wealth + credit, or use expenditure share. The market rule should perhaps be x_i ∝ E_ik × (1 - F_i)^γ or based on budget constraint. Linear incidence is defensible as a transparent, monotone rule, but not derived from a market-clearing model. It can produce artefacts: if the cut is large, the weighting may force cuts on the poor that exceed their spending?
