# Preregistration: a weighted top tail, business owners and the income wealth pays (S59, part 2: switch WEALTH_TAIL and RETURNS options)

Committed Nov 12, 2026 by S59, before either switch is built and before any run of the rows below. Engine: compassionism-simulation at commit `850820ca2be910a96afc8f28b93c380c03b87392` (main); the switches land on branch `claude/s59-tail-returns` from it, off by default (guard test identical). The engine commit of the pilot is recorded in an amendment before the pilot runs.

Part 1 (`prereg/2026-11-s59-asset-classes.md`) split a household's net worth into asset classes. This file covers the rest of S59: the very top of the wealth distribution, who owns private businesses, the money income wealth pays, returns that differ by wealth, the comparison with the Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF) and the Fed's Distributional Financial Accounts, and how the programme's flows reach owners.

## Why
- The engine draws a household's starting net worth from a table of 100 percentiles per household type, which stops at the 99.5th. So the richest households in the model hold about $14-22 million, and the top 1% of adults hold 20.8% of all wealth at the start. In the SCF, for the same families (head aged 25-66 with wage income), the top 1% hold 32.9% by family and 31.5% by adult (computed for this file, `sources/s59_tail.py`; no engine run). Top shares, the wealth Gini and any statement about concentration are therefore measured on a distribution with its top cut off.
- Part 1 gives every household in a wealth group the group's average holdings. In the SCF 14.6% of families hold private-business equity (5% of the poorest group, 87% of the top 1%), and the holdings of owners and non-owners differ.
- Part 1 leaves out the money that wealth pays (interest, dividends, rents and business income that is not self-employment): about $1,070 billion a year for the engine's families against $10,000 billion of wages, most of it at the top.

## Inputs, fixed now (derived before this file from opened sources; no engine run involved)
All in compassionism-simulation `sources/` on the branch: `s59_tail.py` (output `s59_tail.json`) and `s59_capital.py` (`s59_capital.json`).

**The tail.** SCF 2022 public summary extract, five implicates pooled, the survey's weights.
- The cutoff rule (written before it was applied to choose anything in the engine): on a grid of cutoffs from the 90th to the 99.9th percentile of net worth, take the lowest cutoff, up to the 99.5th, from which the Hill estimate of the Pareto exponent at every higher cutoff stays within one standard error of the cutoff's own estimate, and at which the Pareto is not rejected against the more flexible generalised Pareto (twice the gain in log likelihood, scaled to the effective number of families, below 3.84). Result for the engine's families: the 97.5th percentile, $6.16 million in 2022 dollars ($6.78 million in the engine's 2025 dollars), exponent 1.490 (standard error 0.136). For all families: the 98th percentile, exponent 1.466 (0.109).
- The more flexible form: the generalised Pareto fitted above the same cutoff (shape 0.631, scale 0.699 of the cutoff), and fitted from the 90th percentile (shape 0.609, scale 1.155), where the plain Pareto is rejected.
- Share of each household type above the cutoff: couples with children 3.03%, couples without 4.27%, single parents 0.23%, single adults 0.66%. One exponent is used for all four types; the types' own estimates (1.47, 1.53, 2.4 from 20 records, 1.33) are printed as a check.
- Checks, never targets. The Forbes 400 of 2022 (400 entries, $4.005 trillion, lowest $2.7 billion; 35 entries say "& family"; each entry is counted as one family, and the count with and without the "& family" entries is reported): the tail fitted to the SCF alone puts 554 families above $2.7 billion holding $4.08 trillion (295 to 1,043 families over one standard error of the exponent). Adding the Forbes 400 to the SCF raises its top 1% share from 35.10% to 36.76% (+1.66 points; Bricker, Hansen and Volz, *Economics Letters* 2019, report about +1.5). Distributional Financial Accounts, top 1% share: 29.3-30.4% in the quarters of 2022, 32.5% in Q2 2026 (its wealth concept differs from the SCF's: it adds pension entitlements and consumer durables and is scaled to the Financial Accounts).

**Business owners and holdings.** SCF 2022, the engine's families, the seven wealth groups of part 1, each split by whether the family holds private-business equity: the owners' share of the group (4.6%, 5.6%, 10.5%, 11.5%, 20.1%, 45.0%, 87.3%), and for owners and for the others the group's debts over assets, asset shares and fixed-rate share of debts.

**The income wealth pays** (money income for 2021 over the holdings it comes from, by wealth group): interest over nominal assets (0.48%, 0.06%, 0.12%, 0.08%, 0.27%, 0.39%, 1.09%); dividends over shares (0.07%, 1.72%, 0.07%, 0.69%, 0.20%, 0.69%, 1.41%); rents, royalties, trusts and income from businesses other than sole proprietorships over other real estate plus private business (−0.40%, 1.76%, 9.49%, 5.39%, 4.12%, 3.59%, 3.53%). Income from a sole proprietorship or farm (1.5-16% of owners' wage-plus-business income) is not added: it is self-employment income, already inside the earnings the engine draws from the Current Population Survey; it is reported apart from wages.

**Returns that differ by wealth, from the papers (opened Nov 12, 2026).**
- Smith, Zidar and Zwick, *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 2023: in 2016 the interest rate on fixed income was 0.79% for the bottom 90%, 0.77% for the next 9%, 0.91% from the 99th to the 99.9th percentile, 1.52% from the 99.9th to the 99.99th and 3.79% for the top 0.01%. The "3.5 times the average" in the plan is the top 0.01% only; there is no gradient below the 99.9th percentile.
- Campbell, Robbins and Wylde (Equitable Growth working paper, Feb 2025): the top 1% received 45.3% of capital gains and the top 10% 75.7%, 2002-2021, **with people ranked by income including the gains, not by wealth** (the plan's wording did not say); the top 1% by income earned 2.00% a year in gains on owner-occupied real estate against 1.64% for the middle 40%, and 9.06% against 7.55% in total return on rented real estate.
- Fagereng, Guiso, Malacrino and Pistaferri, *Econometrica* 2020 (Norway): direction only; not opened this session and not used as a value.

**A correction to part 1, found while deriving these inputs.** The Fed re-issued the SCF 2022 summary extract on Oct 5, 2026 with every dollar amount in 2025 dollars (a home's value in the extract is 1.09438 times the value given at the interview in the full data set). The engine's wealth tables were computed before that and are right. Part 1's script (Nov 10) read the re-issued file and still applied the 2022-to-2025 price adjustment, so the edges of its seven wealth groups (`RT_EDGES`) are 9.4% too high: a household is placed one group too low if its net worth lies within 9.4% above an edge. The asset shares are ratios and are not affected. The edges are corrected on the branch ($37,073, $196,748, $665,427, $1,855,827, $12,803,724), every `rt` row of this pilot uses them, and the pilot reports part 1's main rows again so the size of the correction is on the record (Amendment 3 of part 1's file).

## What will be built (both off by default; off = identical)
**Switch `WEALTH_TAIL` (study row option `wt`).**
1. In each household type, the `m` households with the highest wealth rank (main: 3 per type, 12 in all of about 300) become tail households. The others keep the type's SCF table, squeezed so that together they stand for the share of the type below the cutoff.
2. The tail of a type runs from the cutoff to the net worth at which one family of that type is left in the United States. It is cut into `m` layers of equal ratio in how rare a family is; tail household j takes layer j and draws its place inside the layer from the tail's own random stream (seed + 600073; one draw per tail household, the same in both runs of a pair). Its net worth is the fitted tail's value at that place (Pareto, main; generalised Pareto, readings).
3. Every adult carries a weight: a tail adult the share of people its layer holds, any other adult a little over 1, summing to the number of adults. **Weights enter measures only.** No rule of the engine reads them: prices, the Source's payout, taxes and every decision are computed exactly as without the tail. Limits that follow, stated in the report: with 12 tail households the model economy holds about three times as many very wealthy households as the population does, so the tail must not move the economy (check 2 below), and a rule that shares money in proportion to wealth cannot be combined with it (the owners readings below run without the tail).
4. Weighted measures (keys `wt*`, and S57's `dm*`, which already read the weight): wealth poverty (adults under $25,000 of real wealth), basket poverty, the wealth Gini (debts kept, and debts as zero), top 0.1%, 1% and 10% shares and the bottom half's, the 99.9th percentile, mean real wealth; the number of tail adults and the sum of weights.

**`RETURNS` options (inside row option `rt`; each off by default, so part 1's readings stay as they were apart from the corrected edges).**
5. `bo`, business owners: each household draws once at the start, on its own stream (seed + 600091), whether it holds private-business equity, with its wealth group's SCF share; its net worth is then split by the SCF holdings of owners, or of the others, in its group.
6. `ci`, the income wealth pays: each year an adult receives, as money, the group's SCF rate on the holdings above (interest on nominal assets, dividends on shares, rents and other business income on other real estate and private business), at the year's values. It is income like any other cash: it enters disposable income, basket poverty and the engine's spending rule, and what is not spent is saved. It is reported apart from wages. Modelling assumptions, labelled: it is not taxed (the model's taxes are on wages and conversions); it does not change hours worked (the survey's hours already reflect it; the programme's effect on it is small under the main reading); sole-proprietorship income stays inside earnings.
7. `ng`, the interest gradient at the very top (acts only with the tail, where an adult's place in the population is known): the interest rate of an adult between the 99.9th and 99.99th percentile is multiplied by 1.52/0.91, above that by 3.79/0.91 (Smith, Zidar and Zwick). Main `'base'`: on the SCF rate only; reading `'all'`: on the inflation pass-through too.
8. `rg`, a reading only: for the top wealth group the real gain on homes is multiplied by 2.00/1.64 and on other real estate by 9.06/7.55 (Campbell, Robbins and Wylde's income ranking read as a wealth ranking: a modelling assumption, which is why it is not in the main reading).
9. The owners rule. Reading of the design (Hub and the engine's record of Duke's answers, Oct 1-2, 2026): the conversion premium earned by a private business that accepts Basic Units is, in the release row, passed to its customers as lower prices, after matching the profit share for its workers; nothing goes to owners (`priv: 'prices'`). The earlier reading (`priv: 'owners'`: owners keep it) pays it to every adult in proportion to positive wealth, because the model had no shares. With asset classes it can be paid in proportion to shares plus private-business equity: option `own: 'eq'` of the surplus split, built here.

**Unit tests, fixed now:** (a) both switches off, and `rt` without the new options, give the paths of the engine before this build exactly (guard test; part 1's rows change only through the corrected edges); (b) the weights sum to the number of adults, and the weighted share of adults in each type's tail equals the SCF share; (c) over many seeds the weighted mean net worth of a type's tail matches the fitted tail's mean to within sampling error, and with the generalised Pareto set to its Pareto case the two forms give the same net worths; (d) both runs of a pair start with the same tail, weights and owners; (e) the main stream's draws per adult-year are unchanged by either switch; (f) the weighted measures at weight 1 equal the plain ones; (g) with `ci`, an adult's capital income equals the rates times the holdings by hand, enters disposable income once, and the three parts of part 1 still sum to real wealth; (h) `own: 'eq'` pays out exactly the owners' pool.

## Questions
1. With the tail, how do the start's top shares and wealth Gini compare with the SCF (same families, by adult) and with the Distributional Financial Accounts? The tail is fitted to the SCF, so agreement with the SCF checks the build; the Fed's accounts and the Forbes 400 are the outside comparisons. Every gap is reported.
2. Does the programme raise or lower concentration (top 0.1%, 1% and 10% shares of wealth, the wealth Gini, the top 1% share of private-business equity) under each design, once the top exists and wealth pays income?
3. Does the tail change the programme's effect on wealth poverty, basket poverty, median real wealth or inflation? (Expected: not beyond noise, since weights enter measures only; a material change is a finding about the build.)
4. What do business ownership and capital income change in part 1's results (wealth poverty, the three parts of the real-wealth effect), and in income inequality?
5. Which tail assumptions move the answers to 1 and 2: the exponent (one standard error either way), the cutoff (99th percentile), the form (generalised Pareto, at the main cutoff and from the 90th percentile), the number of tail households per type (1, 6)?
6. Under the owners reading (owners keep the private premium), by wealth and by equity held, does concentration rise, against the release reading (lower prices)?

## Design
- Rows: the frozen release row and the C2.2 design, each against its own no-programme run (as part 1, S61 and S66: `frozenRow`, `c22Design`, today's safety net, CPS earnings), in Reference, Adverse and Stress; seeds 1-100; 500 adults; 20 years; paired by seed. Free: the session machine or Actions.
- Readings, each on both designs: **off** (all wealth is cash); **p1** (part 1's main reading, corrected edges); **r2** (p1 with `bo`, `ci`); **main** (r2 with the tail: 3 per type, Pareto, main cutoff, `ng: 'base'`); **tail-only** (p1 with the tail); and one change at a time from main: exponent 1.354 and 1.626; cutoff at the 99th percentile (exponent 1.576); generalised Pareto at the main cutoff; generalised Pareto from the 90th percentile; 1 and 6 tail households per type; `ng` off; `ng: 'all'`; `rg`; `bo` off; `ci` off; fixed-rate debt reprices (`chi: 1`, the no-debt-relief reading that must stand beside every `rt` result); real assets follow half of inflation (`ix: 0.5`). Without the tail, from r2: the owners reading by wealth, and by equity.
- Check rows: `wt` with every weight forced to 1 and no change of wealth must equal the row without `wt` on every key.

## Outcomes
Primary: (i) the start's weighted top 1% share of wealth under main, beside the SCF's 31.5% (same families, by adult) and the Fed's accounts; (ii) the programme's effect on the weighted top 1% share at year 20 under main (programme minus no programme, paired). Secondary: the same for the top 0.1% and 10% shares, the bottom half's share, the wealth Gini (both forms), the top 1% share of private-business equity; weighted wealth poverty, basket poverty, median real wealth; programme inflation and the cost of living (check 2); capital income per adult and its share of money income; the top 1% and 10% shares of disposable income; the share of the model's revaluation gains received by the top 1% and 10% of adults ranked by income including the gains (beside Campbell, Robbins and Wylde's 45.3% and 75.7%, as a comparison); the three parts of the real-wealth effect by fifth; business-owner share by income half and top tenth beside the SCF (8.3% and 41.0% holding business equity; the Bulletin's 20%, 14% and "nearly half" also count the self-employed).

## Analysis
Paired differences over seeds with the 2.5th-97.5th percentile interval of 2,000 paired bootstrap replicates (fixed generator), unadjusted, as part 1. Start-of-run levels as means over seeds with the same interval. No significance stars; every reading in the tables.

## Decision rules
- No constant is tuned. The cutoff comes from the rule above, the exponent and rates from the sources; `m`, the form, the cutoff and the exponent are swept and every reading is published. The SCF, the Fed's accounts, the Forbes 400 and the capital-gains shares are comparisons, never targets; gaps are reported, not closed.
- Check 1: the unit tests and the guard test pass. Check 2: under tail-only against p1, programme inflation, the cost of living and the unweighted basket-poverty effect differ by less than their intervals; if not, the report says the tail moves the economy and the tail is not recommended.
- Recommendation to S63 (the freeze): `wt` main, `bo` and `ci` become main-row candidates if both checks pass; the readings stay beside them. If a swept tail assumption changes the sign of primary outcome (ii), the report says the direction of the programme's effect on concentration is not established and names the assumption.
- A synergy or interaction is not claimed from these rows (no factorial here). A pilot is a mechanism check at 100 seeds, not a result; the frozen 500-seed batch decides.

## Reporting
compassionism-simulation `dev/reports/s59-tail-returns.md` (plain words), tables in `dev/runs/s59-tail/`; per-seed results in compassionism-sim-results `s59-tail-<engine commit>` with run.txt and SHA256SUMS. Every reading is published, including nulls and the Adverse and Stress environments. Limits stated there: no creditor side (part 1); weights in measures only; one exponent for four household types; the Forbes list counts individuals and sometimes wider families; capital income untaxed and without an hours response; no political influence (the model has no mechanism for it).
